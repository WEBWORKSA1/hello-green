Food scraps and yard trimmings make up a large share of what households throw away; the US EPA estimates they account for more than 30% of it. In a landfill, food breaks down without oxygen and releases methane, a powerful greenhouse gas. Composted, the same scraps become a soil conditioner that helps gardens hold water and grow without as much fertiliser.

## First, check for city collection

Many cities collect food scraps in a green bin alongside recycling. City programs usually accept things home composting cannot, such as meat, fish, bones, dairy and soiled paper, because industrial facilities run hot enough to break them down safely. If yours does, a countertop caddy may be all you need.

## Choose a method for your space

**Backyard bin or pile (houses with a yard).** A simple enclosed bin, a pallet bay or an open pile in a shady corner. Low cost and handles garden waste as well as kitchen scraps. Finished compost typically takes three to twelve months depending on how often you turn it.

**Tumbler (small yards, patios).** A rotating drum on a stand. Easy to turn and tidy, and keeps pests out. Batches finish faster if you stop adding fresh material while one batch matures.

**Worm bin (vermicomposting) for apartments and balconies.** A box of red wiggler worms eats fruit and vegetable scraps and produces rich castings. It fits under a sink or on a balcony, does not smell when balanced, and works year-round indoors. Keep it between about 15–25°C (60–77°F).

**Bokashi (apartments, small kitchens).** An airtight bucket where scraps ferment with an inoculated bran. It accepts cooked food and small amounts of meat and dairy. After about two weeks the fermented material must be buried in soil, added to a compost bin or dropped at a community garden to finish breaking down.

**Community composting.** Community gardens, farmers’ markets and some apartment buildings accept scraps. A freezer bag of scraps dropped off weekly works for anyone.

## What goes in

**Greens (nitrogen-rich, wet):** fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, tea leaves, fresh grass clippings, fresh plant trimmings.

**Browns (carbon-rich, dry):** dry leaves, shredded cardboard and paper, egg cartons, straw, wood chips, sawdust from untreated wood.

**Keep out of home bins:** meat, fish, bones and dairy (they attract pests; use bokashi or city collection), oils and grease, pet waste, diseased plants, weeds that have gone to seed, glossy or coated paper, and anything labelled “compostable plastic” unless your system can handle it (most home bins cannot).

## Getting the balance right

Aim for roughly two to three parts browns to one part greens by volume. Keep a bag of dry leaves or shredded cardboard beside the bin and add a handful every time you tip in scraps. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge: damp, not soggy. Chop large scraps to speed things up, and turn or mix the pile every week or two to add oxygen.

## Fixing common problems

| Problem | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Smells like rotten eggs or ammonia | Too wet, too many greens, not enough air | Add browns, mix it, check drainage |
| Nothing is happening | Too dry, too many browns, or too cold | Add greens and water, turn it, make the pile larger |
| Fruit flies | Exposed fresh scraps | Bury scraps under browns; keep the caddy lid closed |
| Rodents | Meat, dairy or cooked food in an open pile | Remove those items; use a closed bin with a mesh base |
| Worms escaping the worm bin | Too wet, too acidic or overfed | Add bedding, cut back on citrus and onion, feed less |

## Using finished compost

Compost is ready when it is dark, crumbly and smells like earth, with no recognisable scraps. Mix it into garden beds, top-dress lawns, add a few centimetres around shrubs and trees, or blend it into potting mix for containers. Worm castings are concentrated: use a handful per plant or brew a weak “tea” for houseplants.

## The bottom line

Pick the method that fits the space you have, keep a supply of browns next to the bin, and do not worry about getting it perfect. Even an imperfect compost pile beats sending food to landfill.
