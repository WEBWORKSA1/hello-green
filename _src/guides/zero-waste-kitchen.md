“Zero waste” is a direction, not a test you pass or fail. The kitchen is the best place to start because it produces most of a household’s rubbish, and the biggest wins there also lower your grocery bill.

## Start with food, not packaging

It is tempting to begin with glass jars and beeswax wraps. But the food we throw away usually matters more than the wrapper it came in. In the US, the USDA estimates 30–40% of the food supply is wasted. Every wasted meal wastes the money, water, land and emissions that produced it. Cutting food waste is the zero-waste habit that pays you back fastest.

**Plan, then shop.** Check the fridge and cupboards, plan four or five meals, and shop with a list. Leave flexible nights for leftovers.

**Store food properly.**

- Keep herbs upright in a glass of water in the fridge, loosely covered.
- Store leafy greens with a dry cloth in a container.
- Keep apples, bananas and tomatoes apart from other produce; they release ethylene that ripens neighbours faster.
- Keep potatoes, onions and garlic somewhere cool and dark, but potatoes and onions apart.

**Freeze aggressively.** Bread, overripe bananas, cooked rice, grated cheese, chopped herbs in oil, stock, leftover sauces and portions of soup all freeze well. Label the date.

**Run a “use it up” night.** Once a week, cook from whatever needs eating: fried rice, frittata, soup, stir-fry, pasta bake.

**Understand date labels.** “Use by” is about safety; follow it. “Best before” is about quality. Many foods are fine after that date if they look, smell and taste normal.

## Then cut packaging

**Use what you already own.** Old jars, takeaway containers and tote bags are zero-waste tools already. Buying a new set of matching containers is the opposite of the point.

**Carry the basics.** A couple of cloth bags, produce bags and a water bottle cover most trips.

**Buy loose and in bulk where it makes sense.** Loose fruit and vegetables, bulk dry goods if you have a bulk store, larger containers of things you use a lot.

**Choose packaging that gets recycled where you live.** Aluminium cans, glass and paper are widely recycled. Multi-layer pouches and black plastic usually are not. The [recycling sorter]({root}tools/recycling-sorter.html) covers the common cases.

**Make a few staples.** Stock from vegetable scraps, salad dressing, granola and hummus are quick to make and cut packaging.

## Swaps that are worth it

| Instead of | Try | Why it is worth it |
|---|---|---|
| Paper towels | Cloth rags cut from old towels or shirts | Saves money every week |
| Plastic wrap | A plate over a bowl, containers, beeswax or plant-wax wraps | Fewer single-use rolls |
| Sponges that last a week | Washable cloths, wooden brushes | Lasts months, often compostable |
| Bottled water | Tap water, filtered if needed | Large saving for most households |
| Single-serve coffee pods | Ground coffee in a reusable filter | Cheaper per cup, compostable grounds |
| Zip bags | Reusable containers or silicone bags | Pays back after a few months of use |

Swaps that are often not worth it: buying bamboo or glass versions of things that still work, or products marketed as “eco” with no clear reason why. See [how to spot greenwashing]({root}guides/spot-greenwashing.html).

## Deal with what is left

**Compost food scraps.** Vegetable peels, coffee grounds, eggshells and stale bread can become soil instead of landfill gas. Our [composting guide]({root}guides/composting-at-home.html) covers apartments, balconies and backyards.

**Recycle correctly.** Rinse containers lightly, keep recyclables loose, and keep plastic film out of the curbside bin.

**Keep a small trash bin.** A smaller bin makes waste visible and nudges better habits.

## Energy in the kitchen

Cooking and cold storage use energy too. Keep the fridge at about 3–4°C and the freezer at −18°C, check the door seals, use lids on pans, match pan size to the burner, and use a microwave, kettle or air fryer for small jobs instead of heating a full oven. If you are replacing a gas stove, induction is fast, efficient and keeps combustion pollutants out of your kitchen air.

## The bottom line

Waste less food first, use what you own, and only then buy new “zero-waste” gear. A kitchen that throws away less food and buys less packaging is cheaper to run, and the planet notices the difference.
