A professional energy auditor uses a blower-door fan and an infrared camera. You can find most of the same problems with your hands, a flashlight and half an hour. Do this on a cold, windy day for the clearest results, and keep a notepad or your phone handy.

## What you need

- A flashlight
- An incense stick or a thin strip of tissue paper (to see air movement)
- A sheet of paper
- Your last 12 months of energy bills, if you can find them

## Step 1: Read your bills first (5 minutes)

Look at how your use changes through the year. A big winter spike points at heating and insulation. A big summer spike points at cooling, shading and attic insulation. A high, flat baseline all year points at always-on loads: an old fridge or freezer, a pool pump, electric water heating or standby power. Our guide to [reading your electricity bill]({root}guides/read-your-electricity-bill.html) explains each line.

## Step 2: Hunt for air leaks (10 minutes)

Close all windows and exterior doors, turn off fans and the furnace, and light the incense. Move it slowly around likely leak spots. Where the smoke wavers or streams sideways, air is leaking.

Check these places, where different materials meet:

- Window and door frames, and the bottoms of exterior doors
- Electrical outlets and switch plates on exterior walls
- Where pipes, cables and vents pass through walls, floors and ceilings
- The attic hatch or pull-down stairs
- Baseboards and the joint where walls meet the floor
- Recessed ceiling lights below an attic
- Fireplace dampers (is it actually closed?)
- Dryer vents and kitchen exhaust hoods
- The top of the basement wall, where the house frame sits on the foundation

**The paper test:** close a door or window on a sheet of paper. If it slides out without resistance, the seal is not doing its job.

Write down every leak. Most can be fixed with caulk, foam gaskets behind outlet plates, door sweeps and weatherstripping, for under $100 in materials.

## Step 3: Check insulation (5 minutes)

**Attic.** Look in with a flashlight. If you can see the tops of the floor joists, you almost certainly need more insulation. In cold climates, recommended attic levels are often around 30–40 cm (12–16 inches) of loose fill or batts. Look for gaps, compressed areas and missing insulation around the hatch.

**Walls.** Turn off the power, remove an outlet cover on an exterior wall and shine a light into the gap (do not poke anything in). You may see insulation, or nothing at all.

**Basement or crawl space.** Check whether the rim joist (the band of wood on top of the foundation wall) is insulated. It is a common, cheap fix.

**Hot water pipes.** Insulating the first couple of metres from the water heater cuts heat loss.

## Step 4: Heating, cooling and hot water (5 minutes)

- When was the furnace, boiler or heat pump last serviced? When was the filter changed?
- Are ducts in the attic or crawl space? Look for disconnected sections and gaps at joints. Ducts in unheated spaces leak a lot of energy and should be sealed with mastic or foil tape (not cloth duct tape) and insulated.
- What temperature is the water heater set to? Around 49°C (120°F) is enough for most homes.
- How old is each system? Anything over 15 years old is worth planning a replacement for, ideally before it fails in winter.

## Step 5: Lights and appliances (5 minutes)

- Count the remaining incandescent and halogen bulbs, starting with the rooms you use most.
- Note devices with glowing lights or warm power bricks: they draw power all day.
- Check the fridge and freezer seals with the paper test, and look at their age. Fridges more than about 15 years old use far more electricity than new efficient models.
- Look for a second fridge or freezer in the garage. It may be one of your biggest single loads.

## Turn your notes into a plan

Sort your findings into three lists:

1. **This weekend (under $100):** caulk, weatherstripping, door sweeps, outlet gaskets, LED bulbs, a smart power strip, lowering the water heater.
2. **This year ($300–$3,000):** attic insulation top-up, duct sealing, a smart thermostat, rim joist insulation.
3. **Big decisions:** heat pump, windows, solar, battery. Make these after steps 1 and 2, because a tighter home needs a smaller, cheaper system.

## When to call a professional

Book a professional audit if you are planning a major upgrade, if you suspect moisture or mould, if you have knob-and-tube wiring or vermiculite insulation (which can contain asbestos, so do not disturb it), or if a grant program requires a certified assessment. Many utilities offer free or discounted audits, and some incentive programs require one before and after the work. A blower-door test measures exactly how leaky your home is and shows whether your sealing worked.

## The bottom line

Thirty minutes with a flashlight usually turns up a list of cheap fixes that pay back within a year, plus a clear picture of which big upgrades are worth pricing. Do the cheap list first. Then get quotes for the big ones.
