Planet Pulse

Two numbers that sum up the climate story, pulled automatically from NOAA and NASA every six hours.

What the numbers mean

Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is measured in parts per million (ppm): the number of CO₂ molecules in every million molecules of dry air. Before the industrial era it sat around 280 ppm. The Mauna Loa record, begun in 1958, is the longest continuous direct measurement, and it has risen every year since, now by roughly 2 to 3 ppm a year. CO₂ lingers for centuries, so the level keeps climbing until net emissions fall to zero.

Temperature anomaly means the difference from a baseline average, here 1951–1980. Using anomalies rather than raw temperatures lets scientists combine thousands of stations, ships and buoys fairly. A rise of more than 1°C sounds small, but it is a global average across land and ocean, and it brings more frequent heatwaves, heavier downpours and rising seas.

What you can do with it

The curve bends when emissions fall, and household energy is a big share of emissions. Start with your own number on the footprint calculator, then tackle the largest slice: usually heating, driving or flying.
