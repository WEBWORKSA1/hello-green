Is solar still worth it in 2026? The math after the US tax credit ended

The 30% federal homeowner credit is gone, but many roofs still pay back. Here is how to tell whether yours does, and how to compare quotes.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 5 minute read.

Quick facts

  • The US homeowner solar credit (Section 25D) ended for systems installed after 31 December 2025
  • Leases and power purchase agreements are owned by companies, which may still claim a business credit
  • Payback depends mostly on your electricity price, sun hours and how exported power is credited
  • Compare quotes on price per watt, equipment and warranty, not monthly payment alone

For a decade the first line of every US solar sales pitch was “30% back from the federal government.” That line is gone. The law signed on 4 July 2025 ended the Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) for systems installed after 31 December 2025, and the IRS confirms there is no credit for expenditures after that date. If your system was finished in 2025, you can still claim it on your 2025 return and carry forward any unused amount.

So is solar still worth it? For many homes, yes. For some, no longer. The difference comes down to three numbers you can find in an afternoon.

The three numbers that decide it

1. Your price per kWh. Solar saves you whatever you would have paid for the power it replaces. A home paying 30 cents per kWh saves twice as much per panel as one paying 15 cents. Find the “energy charge” or “per kWh” line on your bill, not the total divided by usage, because fixed connection fees do not change when you add solar. Our guide on reading your electricity bill shows where to look.

2. Your sun. Installers talk in “peak sun hours”: the equivalent number of hours a day of full-strength sunlight. Much of the southern and western US gets 5 to 6.5; the Northeast and Midwest roughly 4 to 4.5; the Pacific Northwest and the UK closer to 3. A south-facing, unshaded roof captures most of it. East and west roofs lose around 10–20%; heavy shade can make a roof a poor candidate.

3. How exported power is credited. Most homes use only part of their solar output as it is made. The rest flows to the grid. Under full net metering you get the full retail price for those exports. Under newer “net billing” or export tariffs you may get a fraction, sometimes a quarter. The lower your export credit, the more a battery or shifting usage into daytime matters.

A worked example

Take a home paying $160 a month at 18 cents per kWh in a region with 4.5 peak sun hours. That is roughly 10,700 kWh a year. A system covering 90% of it needs about 7.3 kW, or around 17 panels.

At a typical installed price near $3.00 per watt, the system costs about $21,900. Before 2026, the federal credit would have cut that to about $15,300. Without it, the full price stands unless your state or utility offers a rebate.

If the home uses 40% of the output directly and earns 75% of retail for the rest, first-year savings land around $1,500. With electricity prices rising 3% a year, payback arrives in roughly 13 years, and the system saves around $50,000 over 25 years. That is still a solid return, but it is a different decision from the roughly 9-year paybacks of the credit era.

Change one input and the answer moves. If the same usage cost 28 cents per kWh, first-year savings rise to about $2,300 and payback drops to roughly 9 years. At 12 cents with a shaded roof, it can stretch past 15. Run your own numbers in the solar savings calculator.

What still lowers the price

  • State and utility rebates. Many states, utilities and cities run their own programs. Search your state in the DSIRE database or our incentives page.
  • Leases and power purchase agreements (PPAs). A company owns the panels and sells you the power. Because the owner is a business, it may still claim a business clean-energy credit on qualifying systems, and some of that value can show up in your rate. You save less over 25 years than if you bought outright, and selling a house with a lease can complicate the sale. Read every escalator clause.
  • Competition. Homeowners who compare several quotes routinely see price gaps of 20% or more for similar systems. Three quotes is the minimum.
  • Timing with a roof replacement. If your roof needs work within five years, do it first. Removing and reinstalling panels later can cost several thousand dollars.

How to compare solar quotes

A quote is only comparable if you line up the same things:

What to compare Why it matters
Price per watt (total price divided by system watts) The fairest single price comparison
Estimated first-year production (kWh) Shows whether the design fits your roof and shade
Panel and inverter brands and warranties 25-year panel warranties are standard; check inverter and workmanship cover
Who handles permits, utility interconnection and any rebates Delays here cost you months of savings
Financing terms Compare total cost, not monthly payment

Be wary of quotes that only show a monthly payment, promise your bill will be “zero”, or push same-day signing. A good installer will show a shading analysis, explain the production estimate and leave you time to decide.

When solar is not the right first step

If your home leaks heat, has old single-pane windows or runs on electric resistance heat, efficiency upgrades can cut your usage enough to change the solar system you need. A smaller system is cheaper. Start with a DIY energy audit, then size solar to the home you will have after the fixes.

Renters and condo owners are not shut out: community solar subscriptions, where available, offer a bill credit without panels on your roof, and plug-in balcony panels are allowed in some countries.

Outside the US

The payback math is the same everywhere; only the inputs change. In India, PM Surya Ghar pays a subsidy of up to ₹78,000 for systems of 3 kW and above, which brings many homes to short paybacks. In the UK and much of Europe, lower sun is partly offset by higher electricity prices. In Australia, strong sun and low installed prices make solar one of the fastest paybacks anywhere, although export credits are low, which favours batteries and daytime usage.

The bottom line

Solar in 2026 is a normal home investment rather than a subsidised bargain. If you pay above-average electricity prices, have a sunny roof with 15+ years of life left and plan to stay put, it usually still pays for itself several times over. If your rate is low or your roof is shaded, put the money into efficiency first and revisit solar when prices fall further.
