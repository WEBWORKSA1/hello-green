Home batteries: who needs one and how to size it

A battery can keep the lights on in an outage and squeeze more value from solar, but it does not pay for itself everywhere. Here is how to decide and what size to buy.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 4 minute read.

Quick facts

  • Most home batteries hold 10 to 15 kWh of usable energy
  • A battery earns most where export credits are low or time-of-use rates are high
  • Backing up a whole home usually needs two or more units
  • Australia’s Cheaper Home Batteries Program has cut upfront prices since July 2025

A home battery stores electricity to use later. That simple job can do three different things for you, and which one you care about decides whether a battery is worth buying.

Three reasons people buy batteries

1. Backup during outages. When the grid goes down, a battery paired with the right equipment keeps chosen circuits running: fridge, lights, internet, a furnace fan or well pump. If outages are frequent where you live, or someone at home relies on powered medical equipment, this alone can justify the cost.

2. Using more of your own solar. Without a battery, a typical home uses only part of its solar output as it is generated and exports the rest. Where exports earn a fraction of the retail price, storing midday surplus and using it in the evening is worth real money. This is the main reason batteries have taken off in Australia and parts of California.

3. Beating time-of-use rates. If your utility charges much more in the late afternoon and evening, a battery can charge from solar or cheap overnight power and discharge during the expensive peak. Some utilities and aggregators also pay homeowners to let their battery support the grid on a few peak days a year.

If none of these applies to you, a battery is unlikely to pay back on savings alone.

How batteries are measured

Two numbers matter:

  • Capacity (kWh): how much energy it stores. Most popular home batteries hold 10 to 15 kWh of usable energy.
  • Power (kW): how much it can deliver at once. This decides which appliances can run at the same time. A 5 kW battery can run lights, a fridge and electronics, but probably not a heat pump, an oven and a dryer together.

Also check the round-trip efficiency (most lithium iron phosphate batteries return about 90% of what goes in), the warranty (commonly 10 years, often with a guaranteed capacity of 60–70% at the end), and whether it can charge from solar during an outage, which needs the right inverter setup.

Sizing a battery

Start with what you want it to do.

For backup: list the essentials and their daily use. A modern fridge uses about 1–2 kWh a day, LED lighting perhaps 1 kWh, internet and phones well under 1 kWh, a gas furnace fan 2–5 kWh in cold weather. A typical “essentials” load lands around 5–10 kWh a day, so one 13 kWh battery covers about a day, longer if solar recharges it. Running central air conditioning, electric heating or an EV charger during an outage usually needs two or three batteries and a larger inverter.

For solar self-use: look at how much of your solar you export on a typical day and how much you use after sunset. The battery only needs to bridge the gap between those two. For many homes that is 8–13 kWh.

For time-of-use savings: size to cover your peak-period use, typically a 3- to 5-hour window.

Costs and payback

Installed prices vary by country, brand and how much electrical work is needed. In the US, a single 10–15 kWh battery commonly costs in the low-to-mid teens of thousands of dollars installed; additional units cost less each because the installation work is shared. The federal homeowner credit that once covered 30% of battery costs ended with 2025, though some states and utilities offer storage rebates or pay for grid services.

In Australia, the federal Cheaper Home Batteries Program, which started in July 2025, has reduced upfront prices for eligible small-scale batteries through certificates, similar to the long-running solar scheme. Check the current discount with the Clean Energy Regulator or your installer.

Payback math is simple in principle: yearly savings equal the energy the battery shifts multiplied by the price difference it captures. If a battery moves 10 kWh a day from a 5-cent export credit to a 30-cent evening rate, it is worth about 25 cents per kWh, or around $900 a year. Where the gap is small, savings are small. That is why batteries pay back fastest where export credits are low, and slowest under full net metering.

Batteries vs. generators

A generator costs less upfront and runs indefinitely with fuel, which matters for multi-day storms. It is also noisy, needs maintenance and fuel storage, and must never run indoors or near windows because of carbon monoxide. A battery is silent, starts instantly and works every day, not just during outages. Some homeowners combine a battery with a small generator for long outages.

Questions to ask installers

  1. Which circuits will be backed up, and do I need a new sub-panel?
  2. Can the battery recharge from my solar panels during an outage?
  3. What is the usable capacity and continuous power output?
  4. What does the warranty guarantee about remaining capacity?
  5. Can I join a utility or grid-services program with it?
  6. Can I add solar or more batteries later?

The bottom line

Buy a battery for backup if outages hurt you. Buy it for savings only if your export credits are low or your time-of-use spread is wide, and run the numbers first. If you are getting solar quotes anyway, ask each installer to quote with and without storage so you can see exactly what the battery adds.
