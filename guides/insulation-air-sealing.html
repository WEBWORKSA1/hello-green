Insulation and air sealing: the cheapest upgrades with the biggest payback

Before solar panels or a new heating system, stop paying to heat and cool the outdoors. Where to seal, how much insulation you need and what to do yourself.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 4 minute read.

Quick facts

  • ENERGY STAR estimates sealing and insulating can save an average of 15% on heating and cooling costs
  • Seal first, then insulate: insulation does not stop air movement
  • The attic is usually the cheapest, highest-impact place to start
  • A tighter home needs a smaller, cheaper heat pump

A home loses heat in winter and gains it in summer through two routes: air leaking through gaps, and heat conducting through walls, roofs and floors. Air sealing deals with the first, insulation with the second. Together they are usually the cheapest energy upgrades you can make, and they make every later upgrade smaller and cheaper. ENERGY STAR estimates homeowners can save an average of 15% on heating and cooling costs by sealing air leaks and adding insulation in attics, floors over crawl spaces and basements.

Seal first, then insulate

Most insulation slows heat conduction but does little to stop moving air. Warm air pushed through a gap carries heat straight past fibreglass or mineral wool. That is why professionals seal the attic floor before adding insulation on top of it. Sealing also stops moisture-laden indoor air from reaching cold surfaces where it can condense.

Where to seal

The biggest leaks are usually hidden:

  • Attic floor: gaps around ceiling light fixtures, plumbing stacks, chimneys, electrical wires and the tops of interior walls. Each is small; together they can add up to a hole the size of a window.
  • Attic hatch: weatherstrip the edges and add a rigid insulation board on top.
  • Basement rim joist: the band of framing sitting on the foundation wall.
  • Windows and doors: weatherstripping, door sweeps and caulk at the trim.
  • Penetrations: where pipes, cables, vents and dryer ducts pass through walls.
  • Ducts: joints in ductwork running through unheated attics or crawl spaces.

Materials: acrylic or silicone caulk for small gaps under about 6 mm; low-expansion spray foam for larger gaps; fire-rated caulk or sheet metal around chimneys and flues; foil tape or mastic for ducts. Never seal around recessed lights that are not rated for insulation contact, and never block combustion appliance air supplies.

How much insulation do you need?

Insulation is rated by its resistance to heat flow: R-value in North America, RSI or U-value elsewhere. Higher R is better. Recommended levels depend on climate:

Location Typical attic target
Hot climates (US South) R-30 to R-49
Mixed and cold climates (most of US and Canada) R-49 to R-60
UK loft 270 mm of mineral wool (current recommendation for new installs)

A quick check: if you can see the tops of your attic floor joists, you probably have too little. Most joists are 9–14 cm (3.5–5.5 inches) deep; cold-climate targets need 35–45 cm (14–18 inches) of loose fill.

Which places pay back first

  1. Attic or loft. Easy access, low cost per square metre and big heat loss. Usually the first job.
  2. Air sealing everywhere. Low material cost, high impact.
  3. Rim joists and basement walls. Cheap with rigid foam board or spray foam.
  4. Crawl spaces and floors over unheated spaces.
  5. Walls. Blowing cellulose or mineral wool into empty wall cavities works well but costs more; external or internal wall insulation on solid walls is a major project.
  6. Windows. Replacing windows rarely pays back on energy alone. Seal and weatherstrip first, add storm windows or secondary glazing, and replace when windows need replacing anyway.

DIY or hire a pro?

Good DIY jobs: caulking, weatherstripping, door sweeps, outlet gaskets, attic hatch insulation, rolling out batts in an accessible attic, rim joist foam board.

Hire a pro for: blowing loose-fill insulation (machines are rentable, but pros are fast and thorough), dense-packing walls, spray foam, any attic with vermiculite (which may contain asbestos), knob-and-tube wiring, or signs of moisture and mould. A professional blower-door test before and after shows exactly how much leakage you removed.

Ventilation matters

As you tighten a home, fresh air has to come from somewhere deliberate. Use bathroom and kitchen exhaust fans that vent outdoors, and consider a heat or energy recovery ventilator in very airtight homes. If you have gas, oil or wood appliances, have a professional check they draft safely after sealing, and keep a carbon monoxide alarm on every level.

What it costs and saves

Weatherstripping and caulking a whole house often costs under $150 in materials. Professional attic air sealing and insulation commonly runs a few thousand dollars, depending on size and region, and is eligible for rebates from many utilities and provinces. Savings depend on how leaky you start, but sealing and insulating usually pays back in a few years, makes rooms noticeably more comfortable and cuts the size of the heat pump you will eventually need.

The bottom line

Seal the gaps, then thicken the attic. It is the least glamorous upgrade in this guide series and often the most profitable.
