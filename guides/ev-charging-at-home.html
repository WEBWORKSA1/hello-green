EV charging at home: Level 1 vs Level 2, costs and installation

Most EV owners do most of their charging at home. Here is what you need, what it costs to install and run, and how to avoid paying for an electrical upgrade you do not need.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 4 minute read.

Quick facts

  • A standard outlet (Level 1) adds roughly 5–8 km (3–5 miles) of range per hour
  • A 240-volt Level 2 charger adds roughly 30–50 km (20–30 miles) per hour
  • Charging at home off-peak is usually the cheapest way to fuel any car
  • Load-management chargers can avoid a costly panel upgrade

The cheapest, most convenient fuel for an electric car is the electricity in your own garage or driveway. Most EV owners charge at home overnight and only use public fast chargers on road trips. Getting home charging right makes the difference between an EV that costs a fraction of a gas car to run and one that costs about the same.

The three levels of charging

Level 1 uses an ordinary household outlet (120 V in North America, or the standard socket elsewhere with a portable cable). It adds roughly 5–8 km (3–5 miles) of range per hour. That sounds slow, but plugged in for 12 hours overnight it covers 60–100 km a day, enough for many commuters.

Level 2 uses a 240-volt circuit and a wall-mounted charger, the same kind of supply as an electric dryer. It adds roughly 30–50 km (20–30 miles) of range per hour, enough to refill most EVs overnight from nearly empty. In the UK and Europe, home chargers typically deliver 7 kW on a single-phase supply.

DC fast charging happens at public stations and can add hundreds of kilometres in 20–40 minutes. It is not installed at homes and usually costs several times more per kWh than charging at home.

Do you need Level 2?

Probably, if any of these apply:

  • You drive more than about 60 km (40 miles) on a typical day.
  • Two EVs share one driveway.
  • Your EV has a large battery you want to refill fully overnight.
  • You want to charge only in a short off-peak window.

If you drive little and have an outlet near where you park, Level 1 may be all you need. Many owners start with Level 1 and upgrade later.

What installation costs

The charger itself usually costs a few hundred dollars or pounds. Installation is the variable part. A simple job with a nearby panel that has spare capacity is often a few hundred to around a thousand dollars in North America. Costs rise with long cable runs, trenching to a detached garage, or a panel upgrade, which on its own can cost several thousand.

Avoiding a panel upgrade. Before agreeing to a new panel, ask the electrician about:

  • A lower-amperage circuit. A 32-amp charger on a 40-amp circuit still adds 35–45 km of range an hour, plenty for most people.
  • Load management. Some chargers watch your home’s total demand and slow down when the dryer or oven is running.
  • Circuit sharing. Devices that let the charger and another large appliance take turns on one circuit.

A licensed electrician should do the work and pull any required permit. In the UK, use an installer approved for EV chargepoints; smart-charging rules apply to new home chargers.

What it costs to run

Running cost is simple: kilometres driven, times your car’s efficiency, times your price per kWh. A typical EV uses 15–20 kWh per 100 km. At 18 cents per kWh, 100 km costs about $3. On a time-of-use plan with a cheap overnight rate, it can be half that. A gas car using 8 litres per 100 km at $1.55 a litre costs over $12 for the same distance.

Public fast charging can cost 2–4 times as much as home power, so the share of charging you do at home matters more than almost anything else. The EV vs gas calculator lets you set that share.

Charging smarter

  • Use off-peak rates. Many utilities offer cheaper night-time electricity for EV owners. Schedule charging in the car’s app or the charger’s settings.
  • Charge to 80% for daily use unless your manufacturer says otherwise. It is gentler on the battery, and you rarely need the last 20% day to day.
  • Pair with solar. Some chargers can follow your solar output so the car soaks up midday surplus. This works best if the car is home during the day.
  • Look at managed charging programs. Some utilities pay a small credit if you let them shift your charging away from peak demand.

Renters and apartment dwellers

Ask your landlord or building about installing a charger; some jurisdictions give tenants a right to request one. Workplace charging, curbside chargers and public Level 2 stations near home can fill the gap. A portable Level 1 cable and a reachable outlet covers more driving than most people expect.

Questions to ask an electrician

  1. Does my panel have capacity for a 40- or 50-amp circuit, or do I need load management?
  2. What amperage do you recommend for my car and driving?
  3. Does the price include the permit and inspection?
  4. Is the charger listed or certified for safety in my country?
  5. Can you make it ready for a second EV later?

The bottom line

Home charging is what makes EVs cheap to run. Most drivers are best served by a Level 2 charger on a modest circuit, scheduled for off-peak hours. Get at least two electrician quotes, and push back on panel upgrades until you have asked about load management.
