Where your household’s carbon footprint really comes from

Heating, driving, flying and food dominate most household footprints. Straws and shopping bags barely register. Here is how to find your big four and cut them.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 3 minute read.

Quick facts

  • Per-person CO₂ emissions range from about 2 tonnes in India to around 14–15 in the US, Canada and Australia
  • One long-haul return flight can add several tonnes per passenger
  • Beef has the highest emissions per kilogram of common foods
  • Home energy and transport are usually the two largest slices

A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas released by the things you do and buy in a year, usually expressed in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e). It is easy to spend energy on the wrong things. Reusable straws are good, but they barely move the number. A few choices move it a lot.

The scale of the problem

Per-person emissions vary enormously by country. According to Our World in Data, the average person in the United States emitted about 14.5 tonnes of CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry in 2025. Averages in Canada and Australia are similar, the UK and much of Europe sit far lower, and India is around 2 tonnes. The world average is roughly 4.7 tonnes. Climate pathways consistent with the Paris Agreement point toward around 2 tonnes per person by mid-century.

National averages count emissions where they are produced. A household footprint counts what you consume, including imported goods and flights, so your personal number can differ from your country’s.

The big four

For most households in wealthy countries, four categories make up the bulk of the footprint.

1. Home energy. Heating and cooling are usually the largest share of home energy use, followed by water heating. A home heated with oil or gas in a cold climate can emit several tonnes a year from heating alone. Electricity emissions depend heavily on your grid: hydro-heavy Québec and nuclear-heavy France have very clean grids; coal-heavy grids are dirtier.

Biggest levers: air sealing and insulation, a heat pump, a heat pump water heater, a green electricity tariff or solar.

2. Driving. A typical gasoline car burning about 8 litres per 100 km emits around 185 grams of CO₂ per kilometre, roughly 2.8 tonnes over 15,000 km. Every litre of gasoline burned releases about 2.31 kg of CO₂.

Biggest levers: fewer car trips, an EV or hybrid for the miles you must drive, transit and cycling for short trips.

3. Flying. Aviation emissions add up quickly because the distances are large. A return economy flight between New York and London emits on the order of one tonne of CO₂ per passenger before counting contrails and other high-altitude effects, which add further warming. Two long-haul trips a year can outweigh a year of careful recycling and LED bulbs many times over.

Biggest levers: fewer flights, rail for trips under about 500–700 km, economy rather than premium seats, longer stays instead of more trips.

4. Food. Diet matters more than “food miles”. Beef and lamb have by far the highest emissions per kilogram because of land use and methane from cattle and sheep. Cheese, pork and poultry are lower; beans, lentils, grains and vegetables much lower. Wasting food wastes all the emissions that went into growing it.

Biggest levers: eat less beef and lamb, waste less food, compost scraps.

Everything else

Shopping, from clothes to electronics to furniture, adds a meaningful share for many people, mostly from manufacturing. Buying fewer, longer-lasting items, second-hand first, beats any recycling program. Waste, water and lighting are real but usually small once the big four are dealt with.

Run the carbon footprint calculator. Quick mode takes two minutes; detailed mode uses the kWh and gas numbers from your bills. Look at your three biggest bars and focus there.

A sensible order of action

  1. Free, this week: lower the thermostat a degree, wash clothes cold, plan meals to cut food waste, skip one car trip a week.
  2. Cheap, this month: seal drafts, LEDs everywhere, a smart thermostat, swap beef for other proteins a few nights a week.
  3. Big, this year or next: heat pump, solar or a green tariff, an EV when your car needs replacing, one fewer flight a year.

Does individual action matter?

Your household footprint is small next to a country’s, and structural changes such as clean grids, building codes and transit matter enormously. But households control a large share of total emissions through heating, driving and buying, and the same upgrades that cut your footprint usually cut your bills. Visible choices, like a heat pump on your street or an EV in your driveway, also spread: people adopt what they see their neighbours use.

The bottom line

Ignore the guilt about small things and go after the big four. Heating, driving, flying and beef are where most household emissions sit, and where most of your savings are hiding too.
