Heat pumps explained: costs, savings and cold-climate performance

How a heat pump moves heat instead of making it, what it costs to install and run, whether it works in a real winter, and how to choose an installer.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 4 minute read.

Quick facts

  • A heat pump typically delivers 2.5 to 4 units of heat for each unit of electricity
  • Modern cold-climate models keep heating well below −15°C (5°F)
  • It replaces both your furnace or boiler and your air conditioner
  • England and Wales: Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant of £7,500 for air-to-water and ground source systems

A furnace or boiler makes heat by burning fuel. An electric heater makes heat from electricity, one unit in, one unit out. A heat pump does something different: it moves heat that already exists outside into your home, the same way a fridge moves heat out of its box. Because moving heat takes far less energy than making it, a heat pump typically delivers 2.5 to 4 units of heat for every unit of electricity it uses. That ratio is called the coefficient of performance, or COP.

In summer the process runs in reverse, so one system heats and cools.

The main types

Air-source, ducted. Replaces a furnace and central air conditioner and uses your existing ducts. Common in North America.

Ductless mini-split. Wall or ceiling units in individual rooms, each linked to an outdoor unit. Ideal for homes without ducts, additions, or for heating the main living area first.

Air-to-water. Heats water for radiators or underfloor heating, and often your hot water too. The usual choice in the UK and Europe where homes have boilers and radiators.

Ground-source (geothermal). Pipes buried in the garden or in boreholes draw heat from the ground, which stays at a steady temperature all year. The most efficient type, and the most expensive to install.

Do heat pumps work in a real winter?

Older heat pumps struggled below freezing. Modern cold-climate models use variable-speed compressors and refrigerants that keep delivering heat well below −15°C (5°F), and some are rated to −25°C or lower. Efficiency does fall as it gets colder, so the seasonal average matters more than the best-case rating. In very cold regions, installers often size the system to cover nearly all hours and keep a small backup, either electric resistance or an existing furnace in a “dual-fuel” setup, for the handful of coldest nights.

Heat pumps in Norway, where a large share of homes use them, are the standard rebuttal to the idea that they only work in mild climates.

What it costs to install

Prices vary widely with the home, the type and the region:

System Typical installed range
Single-zone ductless mini-split US $3,500–$8,000
Whole-home ducted air-source US $12,000–$20,000+
Air-to-water (UK) £10,000–£14,000 before grant
Ground-source Often double an air-source system

Ductwork repairs, electrical panel upgrades and removing an old oil tank can add to the bill. Your quotes should list each item.

Grants change the picture. In England and Wales, the Boiler Upgrade Scheme gives £7,500 off an air-to-water or ground source heat pump, £2,500 off an air-to-air system, and from 21 July 2026 until 31 March 2027 a larger £9,000 grant for eligible homes off the gas grid. Your MCS-certified installer applies for you. In Canada, low-to-median-income households can get no-cost retrofits through the Canada Greener Homes Affordability Program in participating provinces, and many provinces and utilities run their own rebates. In the US, the federal heat pump tax credit ended with 2025, but many states and utilities offer rebates. See the incentives page.

What it costs to run

Running cost depends on how your electricity price compares with your current fuel. As a rule of thumb, divide your electricity price per kWh by the heat pump’s seasonal COP to get the cost per kWh of heat. Then compare it with your fuel:

  • Replacing electric resistance heat or heating oil: savings are almost always large, often 30–60%.
  • Replacing propane: usually a clear saving.
  • Replacing natural gas: it depends. Where gas is cheap and electricity expensive, running costs can be similar. Better insulation, a high-efficiency model or a time-of-use tariff tips it in the heat pump’s favour.

The heat pump calculator does this math with your own bill.

Getting the size right

The most common mistake is sizing by rule of thumb or by copying the old furnace. Oversized heat pumps cycle on and off, waste energy and control humidity poorly. Ask every installer for a room-by-room heat-loss calculation (Manual J in the US, a heat-loss survey to MCS standards in the UK). If an installer will not do one, get another quote.

Seal drafts and add attic insulation first if you can. Every kilowatt of heat loss you remove is a kilowatt of heat pump you do not have to buy.

Questions to ask installers

  1. Will you do a room-by-room heat-loss calculation?
  2. What is the model’s rated capacity and COP at your design winter temperature, not just at 8°C (47°F)?
  3. What backup heat do you recommend, and when would it run?
  4. Do I need an electrical panel upgrade?
  5. Where will the outdoor unit go, and how loud is it?
  6. Will you apply for any grant or rebate for me?
  7. What warranty covers parts and labour, and who services it?

The bottom line

If you heat with oil, propane or electric resistance, a heat pump is usually the best money and carbon decision you can make for your home. If you heat with gas, it is a closer call that comes down to your rates and your house, and it gets better every year that you also need air conditioning. Either way, get the heat-loss calculation, compare at least three quotes, and claim every grant you are entitled to.
