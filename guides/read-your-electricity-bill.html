How to read your electricity bill and find the money leaks

Your bill holds the three numbers every energy decision depends on. Here is where to find them, what the jargon means and the patterns that reveal wasted money.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 3 minute read.

Quick facts

  • Your rate per kWh, monthly kWh use and fixed charges are the three numbers that matter
  • Fixed charges do not fall when you save energy or add solar
  • Time-of-use plans charge different prices by hour
  • A high, flat baseline all year points to always-on loads

  2. Tariff types you might see

  4. Turn your bill into decisions

Most people look at one number on an electricity bill: the total. That number tells you almost nothing about how to lower it. Three other numbers do, and once you can find them you can judge any offer, from a new tariff to a solar quote, in minutes.

The three numbers that matter

1. Usage in kWh. A kilowatt-hour is the energy a 1,000-watt appliance uses in an hour. Your bill shows how many kWh you used this period, and usually a bar chart of the last 12 or 13 months. Note your monthly average and your highest and lowest months.

2. Price per kWh. Look for lines called “energy charge”, “supply”, “generation”, “delivery” or “unit rate”. Many bills split the price into supply (the energy itself) and delivery (the wires that bring it). Add every per-kWh line together to get your true marginal rate: the amount you save for every kWh you do not use. This is the number to put into any calculator.

3. Fixed charges. A daily or monthly “customer charge”, “standing charge” or “connection fee” is paid no matter how much you use. It does not shrink when you save energy or add solar. If your fixed charges are large, dividing your total bill by your usage overstates your real rate.

Tariff types you might see

Flat rate. One price per kWh at all hours. Simple to plan around.

Tiered (inclining block) rates. The first block of kWh each month is cheaper; usage above it costs more. Savings come off the most expensive tier first, so cutting use is worth more than the average price suggests.

Time-of-use (TOU). Prices change by time of day, and sometimes by season. Peak hours are often late afternoon and evening. Shifting the dishwasher, laundry and EV charging to off-peak hours can save money without using less energy at all.

Demand charges. Some plans also charge for your single highest burst of power in the month, measured in kW. Running the oven, dryer, heat pump and EV charger at the same time can set an expensive peak. Staggering big loads helps.

Export or feed-in credits. If you have solar, look for how exported kWh are credited. Under full net metering, exports offset purchases one-for-one. Under net billing or export tariffs, each exported kWh earns less than you pay for one.

Patterns that reveal money leaks

Pull up the 12-month chart and look for these:

  • High baseline all year. If your lowest month is still high, something runs constantly: an old second fridge or freezer, a dehumidifier, a pool or hot-tub pump, electric water heating, or dozens of devices on standby.
  • Large winter spike. Electric heating working hard, often because of air leaks and thin attic insulation. A heat pump and air sealing are the big levers. See insulation and air sealing.
  • Large summer spike. Air conditioning against sun-baked windows and a hot attic. Shading, attic insulation and a more efficient system help.
  • A jump with no lifestyle change. A failing appliance, a stuck water heater element or a well pump running constantly. Investigate quickly.

Turn your bill into decisions

Comparing plans. In competitive markets (many US states, the UK, Australia), compare offers on the total per-kWh price at your actual usage, plus fixed charges, not on a teaser rate.

Sizing solar. Annual kWh tells an installer how big a system you need. Your marginal rate tells you how much each solar kWh saves. Our solar calculator takes both.

Pricing a heat pump or EV. Both add electricity use but remove a fuel bill. The marginal rate, and any off-peak rate you could switch to, decides how much you save.

Checking efficiency fixes. Multiply the kWh a fix saves by your marginal rate. A 30 W device left on all year uses about 263 kWh; at 20 cents that is over $50 a year.

A quick worksheet

  1. Average monthly kWh: ____
  2. Marginal price per kWh (sum of all per-kWh charges): ____
  3. Fixed charges per month: ____
  4. Lowest monthly kWh (your baseline): ____
  5. Highest monthly kWh and which season: ____
  6. Tariff type: flat / tiered / time-of-use / demand
  7. Export credit, if you have solar: ____

With this filled in you can sanity-check any savings claim an installer or retailer makes.

The bottom line

Read past the total. Find your marginal rate, your baseline and your seasonal spikes. They tell you which upgrades will pay, and they are the numbers you should hand to every installer before they quote.
