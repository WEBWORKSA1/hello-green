How to spot greenwashing: labels and claims that mean something

“Eco-friendly”, “natural” and “carbon neutral” can mean almost anything. Here is how to test a green claim and which certifications are backed by real standards.

Hello.Green editorial team. Updated 19 November 2026. 3 minute read.

Quick facts

  • Vague words like “eco-friendly” and “natural” have no fixed legal definition in most places
  • Credible labels are set by independent bodies and audited
  • “Compostable” often means industrial composting only
  • The greenest product is usually the one you already own

On this page

  1. Five questions to ask about any green claim
  2. Labels with real standards behind them
  3. Words to treat with care
  4. Better questions than “is this green?”
  5. The bottom line

Greenwashing is marketing that makes a product or company look more environmentally friendly than it really is. It works because most shoppers do not have time to check claims. You do not need to become an expert; a few habits catch most of it.

Five questions to ask about any green claim

1. Is it specific? “Eco-friendly” and “green” are vague. “Made with 80% recycled aluminium” or “uses 40% less energy than the previous model” can be checked. Regulators in many countries, including the US Federal Trade Commission’s Green Guides and the UK Competition and Markets Authority’s Green Claims Code, warn against broad, unqualified environmental claims.

2. Is it about the whole product or a small part? A bottle labelled “now with 30% plant-based plastic” is still mostly conventional plastic. A “recyclable” label means little if the packaging is not collected where you live.

3. Is it backed by an independent standard? Self-made logos with leaves and globes are not certifications. Credible labels have published criteria and independent auditing.

4. Is it irrelevant? Claiming a product is “CFC-free” is meaningless when CFCs have been banned for decades. Advertising the absence of something that is not allowed anyway is a classic tactic.

5. Is it the lesser of two evils? A more efficient version of a product you do not need is not a green choice. Neither is replacing working items to buy their “eco” versions.

Labels with real standards behind them

Label What it tells you
ENERGY STAR (US, Canada) Appliances and electronics meeting efficiency specifications set by the US EPA
Energy rating labels (UK/EU A–G, Australia star ratings) Standardised energy-use ratings; compare the kWh figure, not just the letter
FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) Wood and paper from forests managed to FSC standards, with chain-of-custody checks
Fairtrade Minimum prices and premiums for producers of coffee, tea, cocoa and more
Certified B Corporation Whole-company assessment of social and environmental performance (not product-level)
GOTS (Global Organic Textile Standard) Organic fibre plus environmental and social criteria in processing
EU Ecolabel Lifecycle-based criteria for specific product groups in the EU
WaterSense (US) Fixtures that use less water while meeting performance standards

No label is perfect, and each covers a specific thing. ENERGY STAR says nothing about whether a fridge was made with fair labour; Fairtrade says nothing about packaging. Use labels for what they measure.

Words to treat with care

“Natural.” Rarely regulated outside food. Arsenic is natural.

“Biodegradable.” Almost everything biodegrades eventually. Without a timeframe and conditions, the claim is empty.

“Compostable.” Often means compostable in an industrial facility at high temperatures, not in a backyard bin. Check whether your city accepts it; if not, it usually goes in the trash, not recycling.

“Carbon neutral” or “net zero” products. These typically rely on buying carbon offsets. Offset quality varies widely, and several investigations have found projects that delivered far less than claimed. A company that publishes its actual emissions cuts, with a credible target, is more convincing than a neutral label alone.

“Recyclable.” Technically recyclable is not the same as actually recycled. Check whether your local program takes it with our recycling sorter.

Better questions than “is this green?”

  • Do I need it at all, or can I borrow, rent or buy it second-hand?
  • Will it last? Can it be repaired? Are spare parts available?
  • What does it cost to run over its life, in energy and consumables?
  • Can I return or recycle it at the end through the maker?

For big purchases, running cost usually matters most. An efficient heat pump, fridge or car saves far more carbon over its life than any difference in packaging.

The bottom line

Look for specific numbers, independent labels and the whole-life picture. Be suspicious of vague words, leafy logos and claims about things that are banned anyway. And remember the least marketed green choice of all: keeping and repairing what you already have.
